# Probability by counting proofs

A logical framework represents a judgment as a type and its derivations as the canonical terms of the type ([Harper, Honsell, and Plotkin, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1145/138027.138060)). A probabilistic judgment, such as "e evaluates to v with probability p", is usually given the number p as an index, and then the framework has to compute with real numbers. The idea is to leave the number out. The constants of a signature carry weights, a derivation has the product of the weights of its constants, and the probability of a judgment is the sum over its canonical derivations. Random choices must be linear hypotheses, as in the linear logical framework ([Cervesato and Pfenning, 2002](https://doi.org/10.1006/inco.2001.2951)), so that no choice is counted twice, and an unrestricted hypothesis may only be filled by a derivation of weight one. Adequacy then says that an encoding preserves weights and not only derivations. The check that a relation is a total function extends as well: modes, coverage in which the weights of the applicable clauses sum to one, and termination with probability one show together that a judgment is a Markov kernel. A paper would give the weighted linear framework, adequacy for a probabilistic lambda calculus and for stochastic grammars, and the check of stochastic totality.

What we know on 22 November 2026. That a probability is a sum over proofs is the distribution semantics of probabilistic logic programming, and it is how weighted models of linear logic compute the probability that a program converges ([Ehrhard, Tasson, and Pagani, POPL 2014](https://doi.org/10.1145/2535838.2535865)). A search for a probabilistic or weighted logical framework in the LF family found nothing. The doubt is that the idea only moves a known semantics into a framework. What would be new is higher-order abstract syntax for weighted judgments, the part that linearity plays, and the totality check.
